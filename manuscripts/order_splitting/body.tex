\begin{abstract}
An order-splitting algorithm is given an integer $N$ and an even integer
$M$ known to be divisible by $p-1$ for every prime $p\mid N$; it must
turn that information into a proper factor.  We give a deterministic
algorithm with bit complexity
\[
 \exp\!\bigl(O(\sqrt{\log N\log\log(N+16)})\bigr)
 \operatorname{bitlen}(M)\operatorname{poly}(\log N).
\]
No factorization of $M$ is required.  The method replaces a full
multiquadratic character average by a sparse positive-semidefinite Gram
average.  A local decomposition retains ramified small primes, permitting
a family consisting only of the origin and coordinate vectors.  We
give the full compatible-root construction, ideal-character assembly,
fixed-degree enumeration algorithm, uniform prime-or-semiprime Burgess
estimate, explicit guards, and deterministic scale search.  The theorem
concerns splitting with a supplied exponent; constructing that exponent
for a general input is a separate computational task.
\end{abstract}

\section{What is being computed?}

For a prime $p$, every nonzero residue satisfies $a^{p-1}=1\pmod p$.
Thus a multiple of all the numbers $p-1$ for $p\mid N$ synchronizes the
endpoint of modular powering.  It does not force the intermediate
square roots of one to agree in the different prime components.
An order splitter finds such a disagreement and extracts it with a gcd.
For example, with $N=77$ and $M=30$, write $M=2\cdot15$.
Then $2^{15}=43\pmod{77}$ and
$\gcd(43-1,77)=7$.  The challenge addressed here is to find a successful
test deterministically for every permitted input, with a subexponential
number of bit operations.

All logarithms without a subscript are natural.  Write
$\operatorname{bitlen}(a)=\lfloor\log_2a\rfloor+1$ for positive integers.
The notation
\[
 L_N[\alpha,c]=
 \exp\bigl((c+o(1))(\log N)^\alpha(\log\log N)^{1-\alpha}\bigr)
\]
is used only to compare asymptotic scales.  The result below is
$L_N[1/2,O(1)]$ overhead, with no optimized leading constant.

\begin{theorem}[Deterministic splitting from a supplied exponent]
\label{thm:os-main}
Let $N\geq3$ be odd with at least two distinct prime factors.  Supply an
even positive integer $M$ in binary satisfying
\[
 p-1\mid M\qquad\text{for every prime }p\mid N.
\]
There is a deterministic algorithm returning a verified integer
$1<g<N$ with $g\mid N$ in
\begin{equation}
 \exp\!\bigl(O(\sqrt{\log N\log\log(N+16)})\bigr)
 \operatorname{bitlen}(M)\operatorname{poly}(\log N)
 \label{eq:os-cost}
\end{equation}
bit operations.  All implied constants are effective and absolute.
The input need not be squarefree and $M$ need not be factored.
\end{theorem}

The promise is divisibility by $p-1$, not necessarily by the exponent of
$(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^*$.  Repeated prime factors are expressly
allowed.  Even integers and perfect powers have immediate separate
reductions; primality testing is not part of this promised splitting
problem.  No assertion is made here that an admissible $M$ is cheaply
constructible from $N$.

\subsection{Sources and the new part of the construction}

The compatible-root and active-character mechanism, and the uniform
quadratic-field sixteenth-moment estimate, originate in the pinned
deterministic Cogentic manuscript \cite{CogDet2026}.  Its supplied-order
bound has overhead
$\exp(O(\sqrt{\log N}\log\log N))$.
The change here is a sparse triangle-compatible Gram average and a
ramification-tolerant local decomposition.  These remove the need to
enumerate every nonzero squareclass of a large vector space, and give
the standard $L[1/2,O(1)]$ overhead in \eqref{eq:os-cost}.

The proof also supplies a simpler independent fixed-degree enumeration
algorithm: quadratic relative norms and at most eight parity targets
reduce a biquadratic generator search to certified weighted
four-dimensional LLL.  It does not assume a free class-group algorithm
or an oracle for a large algebraic integer.
The appendix includes the full shared quadratic package rather than
requiring a reader to reconstruct it from research notes.

\section{The elementary order test}

Write $M=2^tu$ with $u$ odd.  For a test residue $z$, reduce it modulo
$N$.  If it is zero, declare the test trivial.  Otherwise compute
$\gcd(z,N)$ and return it if proper.  For a unit put
$b_0=z^u\pmod N$ and successively inspect
\[
 g_j=\gcd(b_j-1,N),\qquad b_{j+1}=b_j^2\pmod N,
 \qquad 0\leq j\leq t.
\]
Return a proper gcd when it appears; otherwise stop at the first
$b_r=1\pmod N$ and record $(r,b_0)$.
Call this procedure \textsc{SplitTest}.

\begin{lemma}[A failed test synchronizes all local two-parts]
\label{lem:os-test}
Under the promise of Theorem~\ref{thm:os-main}, a nontrivial unit test
either returns a proper divisor or records $(r,b_0)$ such that $b_0$
has exact order $2^r$ modulo every prime divisor and every maximal
prime-power divisor of $N$.  For $r>0$, $b_{r-1}=-1\pmod N$.
The bit cost is $O(\operatorname{bitlen}(M)(\log N)^2)$ using elementary
integer arithmetic.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fermat's theorem gives $b_t=1\pmod p$ for every $p\mid N$.
Thus $\gcd(b_t-1,N)>1$ even if $M$ does not annihilate modulo $p^a$.
If this gcd is not proper it is $N$, so $b_t=1\pmod N$ and the recorded
stage exists.  If $r>0$, the preceding gcd is one.  For each $p^a\Vert N$,
\[
 p^a\mid(b_{r-1}-1)(b_{r-1}+1),\qquad p\nmid b_{r-1}-1.
\]
Since $p$ is odd, $p^a\mid b_{r-1}+1$.  The preceding value is therefore
$-1$ everywhere, and the order of $b_0$ is exactly $2^r$ in every
component.  For $r=0$ it is one everywhere.
Binary powering and at most $t+1$ gcd/squaring steps give the cost.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[An active character exists]
\label{lem:os-active}
There is a modulus $q$, either a prime divisor of $N$ or the product of
two distinct prime divisors, and its Legendre or Jacobi character $\chi$,
such that every tested integer with $\chi(z)=-1$ makes
\textsc{SplitTest} return a proper factor.  Moreover
$P^-(N)\leq q\leq N$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $p_i-1=2^{e_i}m_i$ with $m_i$ odd.
If the $e_i$ are not all equal, choose $p_*$ with maximal $e_i$ and set
$q=p_*$, $\chi=(\cdot/p_*)$.  A negative Legendre symbol makes the
two-part of the order of $z^u$ at $p_*$ equal to $2^{e_*}$, because
$u$ is an odd multiple of $m_*$.  A prime with smaller $e_i$ cannot
have that two-part.  This contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:os-test}.
If all $e_i$ agree, use any two distinct primes and
$\chi=(\cdot/p_1)(\cdot/p_2)$.  A negative product means opposite local
Legendre signs: the nonsquare component has full two-part $2^{e_1}$,
whereas the square component has smaller two-part.  Again a failed test
is impossible.  If $z$ is not a unit modulo $N$, its initial gcd is
already proper, since $\chi(z)=-1$ ensures it is coprime to $q$.
\end{proof}

The algorithm does not know or compute this active modulus.  It is a
device in the proof: failure of every candidate test implies that all
candidate characters at this one unknown modulus equal one.

\section{Compatible square roots from a small prime basis}
\label{sec:os-roots}

Let $q_0=-1$ and let $q_1,\ldots,q_K$ be the first $K$ positive primes.
Assume $P^-(N)>q_K$.  Test all $q_j$ and, if no factor is found, obtain
$z_j=q_j^u$ of synchronized order $2^{r_j}$.
Choose a pivot $j_*$ of maximal $r_j$, put
$r_*=r_{j_*}$ and $\zeta=z_{j_*}$.
Since $q_0=-1$, $r_*\geq1$; also $2^{r_*}<N$.

We express each $z_j$ as $\zeta^{m_j}$ without enumerating that cyclic
group.  Initially set $m=0$.  At step $\nu=0,\ldots,r_*-1$ maintain
\[
 (z_j\zeta^{-m})^{2^{r_*-\nu}}=1\pmod N.
\]
Its half-power is a square root of one.  A proper gcd of that half-power
minus one splits $N$.  Otherwise the half-power is either $1$ in every
prime-power component or $-1$ in every component.  In the first case
keep $m$; in the second add $2^\nu$, which flips that half-power because
$\zeta^{2^{r_*-1}}=-1$.  After $r_*$ steps, $z_j=\zeta^{m_j}$.
There are $O(K(\log N)^2)$ modular operations, or
$O(K(\log N)^4)$ elementary bit operations.

List the nonpivot indices as $i_1,\ldots,i_K$ and set
\begin{equation}
 \delta_r=m_{i_r}\bmod2,\quad
 d^{(r)}=q_{j_*}^{\delta_r}q_{i_r},\quad
 s^{(r)}=(d^{(r)})^{(u+1)/2}
                    \zeta^{-(m_{i_r}+\delta_r)/2}\pmod N.
 \label{eq:os-base-roots}
\end{equation}
Squaring gives $(s^{(r)})^2=d^{(r)}\pmod N$.
For a binary vector $\ell$ write
$\sum_r\ell_r\delta_r=2k(\ell)+\epsilon(\ell)$ and define
\begin{equation}
 d_\ell=q_{j_*}^{\epsilon(\ell)}\prod_{\ell_r=1}q_{i_r},
 \qquad
 s_\ell=q_{j_*}^{-k(\ell)}\prod_{\ell_r=1}s^{(r)}\pmod N.
 \label{eq:os-sparse-roots}
\end{equation}
For nonzero $\ell$, $d_\ell$ is squarefree and not one.  Different
vectors give different signed squarefree integers: their nonpivot prime
factors and possible sign recover the subset.  We have $s_\ell^2=d_\ell$.

For distinct nonzero $\ell_1,\ell_2$, put $\ell_3=\ell_1+\ell_2$.
Canceling their common subset factors in \eqref{eq:os-sparse-roots} gives
\begin{equation}
 s_{\ell_1}s_{\ell_2}=\eta_{12}s_{\ell_3}\pmod N,
 \qquad
 d_{\ell_3}=d_{\ell_1}d_{\ell_2}/\gcd(|d_{\ell_1}|,|d_{\ell_2}|)^2,
 \label{eq:os-compatible}
\end{equation}
where the sign of $\eta_{12}$ is negative exactly when both radicands
are negative.  Thus all required quadratic and triangle-biquadratic
modular embeddings are compatible.

Only requested vectors are materialized.  In the algorithm below they
have at most two coordinates.  In particular, if $q_K\leq R$ then
$|d_\ell|\leq R^3$.  The full $2^K$ family is never constructed.
The product identity for arbitrary vectors has square factor
$|q_{j_*}|^{k(\ell)}$; it need not divide the squarefree basis primorial.
No such stronger divisibility is used here.

\section{The sparse candidate list and its effective guard}
\label{sec:os-scale}

Fix an integer scale $m\geq1$ and use only integer operations to define
\begin{equation}
 L=2^m,\quad a_m=\lceil\sqrt{256Lm}\rceil,\quad
 R=2^{a_m},\quad K=\pi(R),\quad
 S=\{0,e_1,\ldots,e_K\},\quad H=K+1.
 \label{eq:os-scale}
\end{equation}
Generate the primes up to $R$ by an ordinary sieve.
The edge fields have quadratic discriminants at most $4R^3$.
For any triangle, the basis
$1,\sqrt{d_1},\sqrt{d_2},\sqrt{d_1d_2}$ gives an order of
discriminant $256d_1^2d_2^2$, so the field discriminant is at most
\begin{equation}
 D_*=256R^{12}.
 \label{eq:os-Dstar}
\end{equation}
All ramification is at rational primes at most $R$, including 2.
There are at most $H^2$ edge-field occurrences and $H^3$ triangles.
Duplicates need not be removed.

For a field $F$ or $E$ use the actual discriminant to set
$M_F=\lceil\sqrt{|\Delta_F|}\rceil$ and similarly $M_E$.
The norm cutoffs are
\begin{equation}
 Z_F=\max(M_F^3,M_FR),\qquad
 Z_E=\max\bigl(M_E^3,M_E\max_{F\subset E}M_F^2\bigr).
 \label{eq:os-cutoffs}
\end{equation}
The integer
\begin{equation}
 B_m=64D_*^4(R+1)
 \label{eq:os-guard}
\end{equation}
dominates $R$, every cutoff, and $64$ times every field discriminant.
Thus $P^-(N)>B_m$ guarantees all the enumeration and coprimality
hypotheses in the shared quadratic package.

The candidate list at this scale consists of the integers $1,\ldots,R$,
the base tests of Section~\ref{sec:os-roots}, and the modular images
returned by Theorem~\ref{thm:qp-enumeration} for every edge field and
every triangle field with cutoffs \eqref{eq:os-cutoffs}.
Run \textsc{SplitTest} on every image.  Any factor found within an
enumeration routine is also returned after checking divisibility.

\section{Why one candidate must split}
\label{sec:os-contradiction}

Suppose an active modulus from Lemma~\ref{lem:os-active} satisfies
\[
 L\leq\log q<2L,\qquad P^-(q)>B_m,
\]
and suppose every candidate at the scale fails to split.
All rational, unit, and principal-generator characters are then one.
Lemma~\ref{lem:qp-assembly} supplies the compatible ideal characters.
The ramified primes are at most $R$, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:qp-gram} applies with $Y=R$ and excludes only primes
dividing $q$.

Put $X=q^{1/3}$ and $V=\log R$.  For the proof, not the algorithm,
set
\[
 u_0=\lceil(\log X)/V\rceil,
 \qquad Y_1=\lfloor X^{1/u_0}\rfloor\leq R.
\]
As $L\longrightarrow\infty$, $\log Y_1=V-O(\log L)$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:qp-explicit-scale} verifies every following numerical
inequality for the explicit conservative range $m\geq4096$.
The elementary prime-product estimate gives
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_q(X,R)\geq\Psi_q(X,Y_1)
       \geq X(25\log X)^{-u_0}\geq Xe^{-V/8}.
 \label{eq:os-smooth-lower}
\end{equation}
For the last inequality, observe that
\[
 u_0\leq\frac{2L}{3V}+1,
 \qquad V^2\geq256(\log2)L\log L.
\]
Thus $u_0\log(25\log X)/V$ has limit superior at most
$2/(768\log2)<1/8$.  Explicit prime estimates and these inequalities
make the threshold effective.

Also $H-1=\pi(R)\geq cR/\log R$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
 \frac{X(1+\log X)}{H-1}\leq Xe^{-3V/4}
 \label{eq:os-diagonal}
\end{equation}
on that range.  The Gram inequality forces an
edge field with coprime ideal sum at least $\frac12Xe^{-V/8}$.

Every edge discriminant is at most $4R^3$, so
$\log D=O(V)=o(\log q)$.  In particular $D\leq q^{1/200}$ eventually,
and $P^-(q)>D$.  Corollary~\ref{cor:qp-ideal-sum} bounds every such
sum by
\[
 2C_BD^3Xq^{-1/4000}+Xq^{-1/50}(1+\log X)
 \leq Xe^{-(\log q)/8000}
\]
on the same range.  Since $V=o(\log q)$, and explicitly by
Lemma~\ref{lem:qp-explicit-scale}, this
contradicts the lower bound.  A candidate must therefore split.
No unproved equidistribution or smoothness hypothesis occurs in this
argument.

\section{The complete deterministic algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[Supplied-exponent splitter]
\label{alg:os-full}
Input $N,M$ with the promise of Theorem~\ref{thm:os-main}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Put $n=\operatorname{bitlen}(N)$ and form the numerical parameters
      $L,R,D_*,B_m$ in \eqref{eq:os-scale}--\eqref{eq:os-guard} for
      $1\leq m\leq\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor$.
      Let $B_{\max}$ be the largest guard, or 2 if the list is empty.
      Prime generation and the value $K$ are deferred until a scale is
      actually executed.
\item Trial-divide $N$ by the integers from 2 through
      $\min(B_{\max},N-1)$, returning a proper divisor if found.
\item For each scale, construct the compatible base roots or return
      the factor found during their construction.  Enumerate its edge
      and triangle fields, run the finite enumeration routines at
      \eqref{eq:os-cutoffs}, and apply \textsc{SplitTest} to every
      listed image and every integer $1\leq a\leq R$.
\item If no factor has been returned, finish by ordinary trial division
      through $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$.
\end{enumerate}
Every reported factor is checked by exact division.
\end{algorithm}

Step 4 makes the program total on its promised inputs without requiring
the huge effective analytic threshold to be computed at run time.
It is used only for finitely many input integers.  To prove this,
suppose the first trial division finds no factor.  Then
$P^-(N)>B_{\max}$.  Its active modulus $q$ obeys
$q\geq P^-(N)$, so $\log q$ tends to infinity with $N$ in this branch.
The scale
$m_* =\lfloor\log_2\log q\rfloor$ is eventually positive and belongs
to the loop, since $\log q\leq\log N<n$.
It has $B_{m_*}\leq B_{\max}<P^-(q)$ and lies in the contradiction
range of Section~\ref{sec:os-contradiction}.  That iteration succeeds.
The threshold is explicit: $m_*\geq4096$ suffices.
For instance, after a failed initial trial division,
$\log q>\log B_{\max}>\sqrt n$; thus
$n\geq2^{8192}$ is more than enough.  This enormous bound only
certifies that the fallback range is finite and effective.

At each scale $\log B_m=O(V)$ and $H\leq R+1$.
Trial division, prime generation, the $H^3$ triangle calls, and each
polynomial-in-discriminant/cutoff enumeration cost
$\exp(O(V))\operatorname{poly}(\log N)$, apart from the supplied
exponent factor in the order tests.  The root-construction cost has the
same bound by Section~\ref{sec:os-roots}.  Finally
\[
 V=O(\sqrt{Lm})
   =O(\sqrt{\log N\log\log(N+16)})
\]
over all included scales, and their number is $O(\log\log N)$.
This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:os-main}.  Reading or powering with a long
supplied exponent is genuinely charged by
$\operatorname{bitlen}(M)$; a succinct informal description of $M$
does not replace that input in this theorem.

\section{Scope, verification, and the next mathematical obstacle}

The proof is an ordinary mathematical proof, not a claimed formal
verification.  The accompanying exact programs check the local
ramification matrix decompositions, selected finite-field identities,
the exponent arithmetic in the Burgess calculation, and small
coefficient-chain examples.  They do not constitute a benchmarked
implementation of the complete number-field enumeration or the full
splitting algorithm.  The algorithms above specify those components and
their bit costs independently of such finite checks.

The sparse average balances the number of fields against the fraction of
smooth integers.  This balance still has a square-root logarithmic
scale.  Within the same diagonal-loss argument, nonuniform weights have
effective cardinality
$(\sum_a w_a)^2/\sum_a w_a^2\leq H$; they cannot create more independent
averaging mass than the number of vertices.  Reducing the first
$L$-parameter requires additional positive mass, a different diagonal
control, a family used without explicit enumeration, or a substantially
different relation constructor.  No general factoring bound below
$L[1/3]$ follows from the supplied-exponent theorem.
